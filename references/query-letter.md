# Query Letter Writer

Write query letters, synopses, and pitch documents for fiction submissions.
This is the most formulaic sub-skill in The Manuscript — agents have rigid expectations,
and deviation kills your chances before they read a word of your prose.

---

## What Writers Get Wrong

Query letters fail for a handful of predictable reasons:
- Opening with rhetorical questions ("Have you ever wondered what it would be like to...")
- Leading with comparisons ("My book is like Harry Potter meets The Godfather")
- Describing the book's themes before its plot
- Burying the hook in paragraph three
- Word count way over or under
- Forgetting to include basic metadata (genre, word count, comps)
- Synopsis that summarizes every subplot instead of the spine

This skill exists to avoid all of the above.

---

## Modes

### Mode 1: Write a Query Letter

Writer provides: title, genre, word count, a description of the story (can be rough),
and optionally: comparable titles, their bio/credentials, and the agent's name.

You produce a ready-to-send query letter.

### Mode 2: Critique a Query Letter

Writer provides their existing query. You diagnose what's working and what isn't,
with specific line-level feedback.

### Mode 3: Write a Synopsis

Writer provides the full story (outline or manuscript). You write a synopsis.
Ask whether they need a 1-page, 2-page, or full synopsis — agents specify.

## Query Letter Structure

A query letter has four parts. In this order. Every time.

### 1. The Hook (1–2 sentences)

The distilled story. Not the theme. Not the premise. The *story* — character + want +
obstacle + stakes. This is the hardest sentence to write and the most important.

**Formula (not a template — internalize it):**
[Character] wants [specific goal] but [specific obstacle], and if they fail, [specific consequence].

**Good hook:** "When seventeen-year-old Amara's brother is conscripted into the army
that killed their father, she has three weeks to infiltrate the capital's military census
office and forge his exemption — or watch the war take the last family she has."

**Bad hook:** "This is a story about family, sacrifice, and the cost of survival in
a world where war is inevitable."

### 2. The Story Summary (2–3 paragraphs)

Expand the hook into a compressed narrative arc. Cover: the inciting incident, the
protagonist's goal and obstacle, the escalating stakes, and the climax (yes — include
the ending in the query; don't write "you'll have to read to find out").

**Rules:**
- Third person, present tense, regardless of the manuscript's POV or tense
- One protagonist. Maybe two if it's dual POV and both are equally essential.
- No subplots unless they're load-bearing for the main story
- No character names unless essential — too many names in a query is confusing
- End the summary on the central dramatic question, not the resolution
  (save resolution for the synopsis)

### 3. The Metadata Line (1 sentence)

[TITLE] is a [word count]-word [genre] novel[, standalone with series potential / first
in a planned trilogy / etc.][. It will appeal to readers of COMP TITLE and COMP TITLE].

**Comp titles:**
- Published in the last 3–5 years ideally (not older, not so recent it hasn't settled)
- Similar in *tone and audience*, not just premise
- Real published novels, not films or TV shows (unless the agent specifically welcomes those)
- Don't compare to mega-bestsellers (saying "fans of Harry Potter" tells the agent nothing)

**Word count guidelines by genre:**
- Literary fiction: 70,000–100,000
- Commercial / upmarket fiction: 80,000–100,000
- Thriller / mystery: 70,000–90,000
- Fantasy / sci-fi: 90,000–120,000 (debut; established authors go longer)
- Romance: 70,000–100,000
- YA: 50,000–80,000
- MG: 30,000–55,000

### 4. The Bio (1–2 sentences)

Relevant credentials only: publication history, relevant professional experience,
degrees that matter for the subject, membership in writing organizations (HWA, MWA,
RWA, SFWA). If you have no credentials, a single sentence is fine:
"I am a [location]-based writer and this is my debut novel."

Do not: apologize for being a debut, mention that your friends loved it, explain
how long it took to write, or describe your personal connection to the themes.

## Full Query Letter Template

```
Dear [Agent Name],

[Hook — 1–2 sentences]

[Story paragraph 1 — setup, protagonist, inciting incident]

[Story paragraph 2 — escalation, stakes, central conflict]

[Optional story paragraph 3 — if needed for clarity; climax approach]

[TITLE] is a [word count]-word [genre]. [Comp sentence if using comps.]
[Series note if applicable.]

[Bio — 1–2 sentences]

Thank you for your time and consideration.

[Name]
[Contact info]
```

## Synopsis Structure

A synopsis covers the *entire* story including the ending. It is not a back-cover blurb.

**1-page synopsis (~500 words):**
- Setup + inciting incident: 1 paragraph
- Act 2 escalation: 2–3 paragraphs
- Climax + resolution: 1 paragraph
- Emotional/thematic landing: 1 sentence

**2-page synopsis (~1000 words):**
Same structure, more room for key subplots and character development.

**Rules:**
- Third person, present tense
- Character names in ALL CAPS on first mention only (standard convention)
- Spine only — cut subplots that don't directly affect the main plot
- Don't editorialize ("In a heart-pounding climax..." — just describe what happens)
- Must include the ending. Always.

## Critique Format

When critiquing an existing query:

```markdown
## Query Critique

**Hook:** [Works / Needs work] — [Specific feedback]
**Summary:** [Works / Needs work] — [Specific feedback]
**Metadata:** [Complete / Missing: ___]
**Bio:** [Fine / Overlong / Underdeveloped]
**Overall:** [1–2 sentences on the query's main strength and main problem]

**Priority fix:** [The single most important change to make]

**Line-level notes:**
> "[Exact quoted line]" — [Specific issue and suggestion]
```

## Critical Rules

**No rhetorical questions.** Ever. Not as the opening, not anywhere.

**No theme statements in the hook.** "This is a story about what it means to be human"
belongs in a college essay, not a query letter.

**Present tense, third person in summary.** Even if the book is first person past tense.

**Always include the ending in a synopsis.** Agents who request synopses want to know
if the story works. "Find out in the book!" is an automatic pass.

**Personalize the salutation.** "Dear Agent" is worse than no query at all.
If the writer doesn't know the agent's name, help them find it.
